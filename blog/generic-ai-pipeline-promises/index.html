Generic AI Pipeline Promises All Sound the Same

Buyers hear 'AI that builds pipeline and closes deals faster' every day. A specific solution is what gets a reply, a shortlist spot, and an AI citation.

4 Nov 2026By Colin DowlingPart of The BoFu Map

Key takeaways

  • If your line could be forwarded from a stranger's inbox, it is not a position.
  • Buyers are not tired of AI. They are tired of the same sentence from people they do not know.
  • A solution names the job, the person, the workflow, and the limit.
  • Answer engines cannot cite a claim that every vendor makes in the same words.

If I forwarded you the emails a careful buyer got this morning, you would not be able to tell the senders apart. That is the whole problem with the way most AI products are marketed right now.

A healthcare imaging executive I interviewed on the Modern Business Life podcast described his inbox without any drama. He gets email every day, literally every day, from people he does not know. He does not know who they are. And it is always AI, AI, AI. Build my pipeline. Close deals faster. His conclusion was short. You need to come to the table with a solution.

He is not anti-AI. Later in the same conversation he described a job he would actually want it to do, if he were running a large sales organization again, the kind of company he named from his own career, such as GE, Philips, or Siemens. Dig into the funnel. Pull old leads back out. Deduplicate contacts. Clean the go-to-market data. Organize campaigns that are actually aimed. He was quicker to see that job in a big sales org with a messy history than in a small startup. He did not stop at "build pipeline."

The inbox test

Read your homepage headline, your outbound opener, and the first line of your comparison page out loud, one after another. Then read them as if they arrived from a company the reader has never heard of.

If a buyer could not tell which sentence is yours, you have the category sentence, not a position. I have written about this as me-too positioning: experts and buyers both pass over you when you sound like everyone else. AI gave that habit a shared script. Put a stranger's logo on the sentence you are proud of. If it still works, it is not yours.

Come to the table with a solution

A solution is boring in the best way. It has a person, a pile of work, and a limit.

  • The person. Not "sales teams." The operator who feels the mess. In his example, someone sitting on years of funnel data, duplicate contacts, and leads nobody has touched.
  • The job. A verb you could watch. Pull the old leads. Merge the duplicates. Show which accounts are real. That is different from "unlock pipeline."
  • The before and after, in their words. What Monday looks like now. What it looks like if the job is done. If you cannot say it without an adjective, you are not done.
  • The limit. Where this is the wrong tool. He already drew one: a big, messy commercial org is a better fit for that cleanup than a startup that barely has data yet. Putting the limit on the page is how a buyer trusts the rest.

This is the same standard I use for content buyers still trust when they assume AI wrote it. Specific situations, a real constraint, a point of view. The generic line has none of those. Do not save the specific version for the demo. The buyer who is tired of the inbox will not book a call to find out whether you are different.

Make the decision pages impossible to confuse

Bottom-of-funnel pages are where sameness becomes expensive. A buyer on your alternatives page is trying to separate you from two other tabs. If all three open with AI and faster pipeline, they will close the tabs and stay with whoever they already use.

Rewrite those pages around the job:

  • One page for the actual job, such as cleaning a stale funnel, not for "AI sales."
  • A comparison that admits where the other approach is the better fit, including doing it by hand or staying with the incumbent.
  • A best-fit page that says which teams have the data mess, and which teams are too early for it.
  • Proof that matches the job. A before and after a buyer in that seat would recognize, not a logo next to a slogan.

The BoFu Map is how you find the searches that punish generic copy. The bottom-of-funnel list in a category is finite: alternatives, versus, pricing, best-for, switching. "AI to clean duplicate contacts in a CRM" is a search a person types. "AI that closes deals faster" is a billboard. Build the page for the search.

Then ask the answer engines in the buyer's job language, not in your tagline. If the answer describes a category and never names you, the pages are still written in the category sentence. When your descriptions say nothing distinctive, the model cannot describe you either.

What to do this week

Pull the last ten outbound emails and the homepage hero. Underline every promise that does not name a person, a job, or a limit. Those lines are the ones a buyer files with the daily pile.

Replace one of them with a job a real customer has described, the way he described digging through an old funnel and deduping contacts. Read the new line to someone outside the writing meeting. If they say "AI for sales," you are still in the pile. Then do the same to the first paragraph of your alternatives page. He did not say he throws the emails away. He said he does not know who sent them. Unknown is not a shortlist.

A worked example

Two companies sell AI to sales leaders. Company A's site says it uses AI to build pipeline and close deals faster. Company B's site says it digs through an old, duplicated funnel, pulls leads nobody has worked, merges the contacts, and helps a large sales org run campaigns off a list that is finally clean. It also says a startup with a thin database will not get much from it.

Company A is the email he gets every day. Company B came to the table with a solution. Only Company B gives an answer engine a sentence worth attaching to a name. The product behind Company A might be better. The page never said anything the buyer had not already skimmed past.

Common mistakes

  • Adding AI to a sentence you already used. The technology is not the difference. The job is.
  • Saving the specifics for sales. Buyers who are sorting vendors will not wait until a call to find out what you do.
  • Proof that repeats the slogan. A quote that says "it helped us build pipeline" is the same sentence in a customer's mouth.
  • Refusing the limit. If you fit everyone, a cautious buyer assumes you fit no one they know.

Questions

Why do AI pipeline promises get ignored?

Because serious buyers hear the same sentence every day, from people they have never met: AI, build my pipeline, close deals faster. The words do not describe a job, so there is nothing to answer. A specific solution gives them a reason to tell you apart from the rest of the inbox.

What is the difference between an AI claim and a solution?

An AI claim names the technology and a vague outcome. A solution names whose workflow changes, what work gets done, what it replaces, and where it does not fit. Cleaning duplicate contacts in a large, messy funnel is a solution. Building pipeline faster is a claim.

How does generic messaging hurt bottom-of-funnel content?

Comparison and alternatives pages only work if a buyer can see a difference. If your page and your competitor's page both promise AI-driven pipeline, the reader learns nothing and defaults to the incumbent, the cheapest option, or no decision. Specific jobs and honest limits are what make those pages worth ranking.

Can generic AI copy still get you cited by answer engines?

Rarely under your name. Models repeat distinctive, checkable sentences. A line every vendor uses gets attributed to the category, or to whoever has the most third-party mentions. If you want ChatGPT or Perplexity to name you, the page has to say something only you can say, in words a buyer would actually search.
