Sell the Outcome for Your Customer's Customer

Your buyer has to explain the purchase to their own customer. Bottom-of-funnel content should tell that story, or the champion has to invent it.

4 Nov 2026By BoFu, BabyPart of The BoFu Map

Key takeaways

  • The person who signs is rarely the person who feels the outcome.
  • Name the inconvenience your buyer's customer no longer has to live with.
  • Feature pages make the champion invent the story. Outcome pages let them forward it.
  • Answer engines get asked the downstream question. Give them a sentence they can cite.

Your buyer is not the end of the story. They have a customer of their own, and that person is often the reason the deal happens or dies. If your content never mentions that person, you are asking your champion to invent the most important sentence in the internal meeting.

A dental tech executive interviewed on the Modern Business Life podcast kept coming back to this. He was talking about a same-day crown. The practice preps the tooth, scans it, and sends it to the cloud. The design comes back in less than a minute. They print it in less than ten minutes. The patient gets a final crown in less than 45 minutes. Then he stepped past the workflow and named the person it is actually for. Think of the patient who has to take a day off work to come to the dentist. That inconvenience, he said, is sometimes enough for the patient to decide they'll wait. They can't do it.

The dentist is the buyer. The patient is the one who feels the day.

Your champion has to retell this

In a B2B deal, the person you sell to rarely gets to decide alone. They have to explain the purchase to someone who will never sit through your demo. A partner. A finance lead. Their own customer. If the only language on your site is features, integrations, and "platform," that person leaves the meeting with nothing to say.

We see this when teams can't name the problem they solve in the buyer's words. They can name the product. They cannot name the afternoon it gives back to someone downstream.

The fix is one concrete outcome, told from the far side of the buyer. What did their customer have to do before? What do they do after? What inconvenience was large enough that people quietly opted out?

That is also how you find the searches worth building pages for. The BoFu Map is the finite list of bottom-of-funnel terms in a category: alternatives, versus, pricing, best-for, switching, integrations. Several of those are the downstream question in disguise. If your pages only answer the feature version, you miss the search the champion actually types.

Write the outcome in the end customer's day

Start from a real workflow, the way he did. Not a category. A sequence.

  • What the buyer does. The steps they stop doing, or do in one visit instead of two.
  • What their customer feels. Time, inconvenience, a second appointment, a delay they have been quietly accepting.
  • The number you can stand behind. He did not say "much faster." He said under a minute for the design, under ten minutes to print, under 45 minutes to a final crown. Use a number you would say on a call with the buyer's most skeptical colleague in the room.
  • The opt-out you are preventing. Sometimes the cost of the old way is not a complaint. It is a customer who decides to wait, or to go elsewhere, because the inconvenience is too much. That is a buying reason. Put it on the page.

Then check the cast. Personas built around buying drivers should include the person who never logs into your product. If every persona is a job title on the buying committee, you will write committee content and skip the page a champion can forward.

Make it the spine of the decision pages

This belongs on the pages people read when they are choosing, not only on the homepage.

The comparison page should say what changes for the end customer, not only which features differ. The alternatives page should say what that customer loses if the team keeps the old workaround. A practice whose patients will not take another day off is a different sale from one whose patients can come back tomorrow.

Buyers who don't know your category exists are often searching in that downstream language already. They are not looking up your product name. They are looking up their customer's problem, and the homegrown fix they are tired of. Meet them there.

Do the same for answer engines. A buyer who asks how to stop their customers coming back for a second visit will not get a useful answer from a page about your architecture. Write the workflow, the time, and whose day changes in the first two sentences, so a model can lift it. And keep the condition next to the number. Under 45 minutes is only defensible if the page says what the practice has to have in place, and where it does not hold.

What to do this week

Take your most-visited product page. Highlight every sentence that describes your buyer. Then highlight every sentence that describes your buyer's customer. If the second highlight is empty, that is the rewrite.

Call two recent customers and ask what their customer stopped having to do. Write down their words. Do not upgrade them. If the answer is about your dashboard, you do not have the outcome yet.

A worked example

A company sells equipment into professional practices. The old page says the product is powered by AI, prints in-office, and improves efficiency. A dentist can nod at all of that and still have nothing to tell a patient.

The rewrite leads with the patient who used to take a day off and often did not come. Then it gives the workflow the executive described: prep, scan, a design back in under a minute, a print in under ten minutes, a final crown in under 45 minutes, one visit. Then it says what the practice has to have in place for that to be true. The dentist now has a sentence they can say at the front desk. That is the sentence that sells the product when you are not in the room. The same shape works outside dentistry. If you cannot name the end customer and what they stop having to do, you are still selling to yourself.

Common mistakes

  • Stopping at the buyer's convenience. Faster for your user is good. It is not the same as better for their customer.
  • Using a vague outcome. "Delight" and "experience" do not survive a buying meeting. A day off work does.
  • Hiding the condition. If the outcome depends on a workflow the buyer has to adopt, say so next to the number.
  • Leaving it off the comparison page. That is the page the champion has open while they explain you.

Questions

What does it mean to sell the outcome for your customer's customer?

It means describing the result in the life of the person your buyer serves, not only in the life of the buyer. A dentist buys a faster crown workflow. The patient is the one who no longer has to take a day off work. Your pages should make that second outcome obvious.

Why do feature pages stall late in a B2B deal?

Late in a deal, the champion has to explain the purchase to a boss, a finance partner, or their own customer. A feature list does not survive that meeting. A plain account of what changes for the end customer does. If your site never says it, the champion has to make it up, and most of them will not.

How should this show up in bottom-of-funnel content?

On the comparison, the alternatives page, and the best-fit page, add a short section on what the buyer's customer experiences before and after. Use a real workflow and a real inconvenience. Keep the numbers your customer can stand behind, and say whose day actually changes.

Does this help you get cited by AI answer engines?

Yes, when buyers ask what a product does for their customers, not what features it lists. A page that answers in one or two concrete sentences gives ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews something specific to repeat. A page that stops at the feature does not.
