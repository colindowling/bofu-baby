A buyer close to a decision needs a reason to bet their reputation on you. When the long study does not exist yet, that reason is proxy proof: the tests, the simulations, the early users, and the people those buyers already listen to.

I heard the buyer's question in one line from a dental tech executive I interviewed on the Modern Business Life podcast. His buyers are dentists. How do you get a dentist to say it's good enough?

He did not pretend the long study was sitting in a drawer. The trade-off, as he put it, is that you don't have five years of research. You have bench testing. You have a simulated study. You have the weight of big data on the properties of the material. After that, the job changes. It becomes getting the right voices. The dentists with the strong voices who can look at that evidence and say, I get it.

That is a bottom-of-funnel problem, even though it started in a clinical market. The same gap shows up in software, equipment, and services. Your best buyers are ready to choose. The proof they wish they had takes years. The decision will not wait.

## Name the gap before someone else names it for you

The worst version of this is a page that talks as if the long study exists. Clinically proven. Trusted by leaders. Proven results. If a buyer, a technical reviewer, or an answer engine goes looking for the study and finds a slogan, you lose the deal and the citation in the same afternoon.

The honest version is shorter and stronger. We do not have five years in the field yet. Here is what we do have, and here is what it can and cannot tell you.

That page gets reused. A champion can forward it, and an answer engine can lift it, because the sentence has a boundary. [How AI citation is measured](/#aeo) starts from the same idea: a model names you when the page answers in words it can repeat without inventing the rest. Specifics and limits are also [what still earns trust when everyone assumes AI wrote it](/blog/content-buyers-trust-ai-era/).

He was clear about the cost of waiting. If his industry had to sit still for a five-year clinical study before using a new material, it would never take advantage of the advances already in hand. Research, he said, is moving at light speed compared with where it was. That is not a reason to skip proof. It is the reason the proxy has to stand in public.

## Build the proxy out of evidence you actually have

Proxy proof is not a softer word for a claim. It is a stack, and each layer has to be real.

- **The test you can describe.** Bench testing, a simulated study, a monitored pilot, a before-and-after on one workflow. Say the method in a few sentences. Say what you measured. Say what you did not measure.
- **The property, not the adjective.** Stronger is not proof. The property you tested is. If you cannot name the property, you are not ready to publish the line.
- **The early use, with the edges on it.** One team, one use case, what changed, what did not. A small true story beats a large vague one. Technical readers will [dismiss a page that hides the edges](/blog/content-technical-buyers-wont-dismiss/).
- **The voice the buyer already trusts.** Not a logo wall. A practitioner who will say, in their words, that they looked at the trade-off and used it anyway. The data gets you into the conversation. The right voice is what lets a peer say it is good enough.

Keep the layers on one page. If the bench note is in a PDF, the pilot is in a deck, and the quote is in a webinar nobody can find, you have fragments, not proof.

## Put it where a deciding buyer and an answer engine both look

Bottom-of-funnel content is where this belongs. Not a thought-leadership post about the future of evidence. A page for the question the buyer is actually asking: is this good enough for a team like mine, and what would I be accepting if I move before the long study exists?

Answer that in the first two sentences. Then show the stack. Then say who it is not for. Put it on the comparison page, next to implementation, and in the words your sellers use. If the page says "simulated study" and the rep says "clinically proven," the buyer believes neither. Pages like that are what we look at when we separate [ranking from getting cited](/blog/ranking-and-getting-cited/). A vague page can still rank. A citation needs a sentence that survives the question: what evidence is there if there is no long-term study yet?

## What to do this week

Pick the claim your homepage is proudest of. Ask someone who knows the product what evidence you actually have, and what you do not. Write both answers in the buyer's language. If that paragraph will not sit on half a page, the claim is bigger than the proof.

Then find one credible user who has already said some version of "I get it." Ask if you can use their words with the limit attached. Do not polish the limit out. The limit is why a peer believes them.

## A worked example

A company selling a new clinical material has no five-year study. The old page says the material is advanced, trusted, and approved by dentists. A deciding dentist learns nothing, and an answer engine has nothing specific to repeat.

The rewrite says there is no five-year clinical study yet. Here is the bench test, here is the simulated study, and here is what the properties look like in the data the company does have. Here is a dentist who teaches in this field explaining why that was enough for their practice, and what they are still watching. Same product. The second page can be forwarded, and it can be cited.

## Common mistakes

- **Borrowing a study you did not run.** "Studies show," with no study, is how technical buyers leave.
- **Waiting in silence.** No page at all tells the buyer you are hoping they will not ask.
- **Logos instead of voices.** A row of customer names is not a person saying they looked at the trade-off.
- **One careful page and a contradictory homepage.** The honest page does not help if every other page outruns it.
