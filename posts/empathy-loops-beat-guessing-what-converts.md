You will not guess your way to a page that converts. The teams that get this right keep a loop running: talk to users, use the product, go see the work, and rewrite the page. The guess is what you publish when that loop has stopped.

A sports-tech product leader we interviewed described the habit in practical terms. The team kept conducting user interviews, using the product firsthand, going on customer visits, and attending conferences. Whatever it took to put themselves in the shoes of their users as they scaled, so they still had a feel for which problems to solve. Those problems change. The loop is how you notice.

Their product serves coaches. The same habit matters when the person in those shoes is a champion trying to get a purchase approved. If nobody has sat in that chair lately, you are guessing what the comparison page should say, and the page will rank for a vague term, get cited for nothing, and convert by accident.

## Why the guess feels like research

The guess starts inside the building. A leadership meeting. The last deal someone remembers. A competitor's homepage, lightly rewritten. The strongest opinion in the room becomes the headline, and the page sounds like you.

Buyers do not recognize themselves in it. The situation is too clean. The objection is the one you wish they had. The proof is a claim about speed with no condition, so a champion cannot defend it in the meeting you are not in.

[Personas built around channels](/blog/personas-buying-drivers-not-channels/) have the same hole. They tell you where someone spends time. They do not tell you what that person is trying to get done, or which sentence they would trust enough to repeat. [Find out what buyers actually say](/blog/branding-research-not-gut-feel/) before you freeze the message. Instinct is a hypothesis. A money page built only on it is a bet, and answer engines will not cite it either. They repeat a real situation. A conference-room guess gives them nothing.

## What to put in the loop

An empathy loop is not a brand workshop you finish. It is repeated contact with the people who live the problem, plus a rule that each round changes a page.

You do not need all four every week. You need them often enough that the copy cannot drift for a quarter.

- **User interviews.** Recent wins, recent losses, and someone who almost bought. Ask what was happening before they searched, what they tried first, and which sentence on your site they did not believe.
- **Using the product firsthand.** Not the happy-path demo. A real task, with the friction still in it. The step your page calls simple is often where a new buyer gets stuck. That step belongs above the call to action.
- **Customer visits.** Watch which screen they live in, who they have to convince, and which word they use for the pain. The word on the page should be that word.
- **Conferences and the rooms your buyers already use.** Skip the badge scan. Listen to how practitioners describe the problem when nobody is pitching. Those phrases show up later in search.

Write the phrases down and do not clean them up. The rough version is what the next buyer types, and what an answer engine can match. A polished restatement of your positioning is what you already had.

[Naming the problem in their words](/blog/name-the-problem-you-solve/) is the point of the notes. Put that language on the matching page in the [BoFu Map](/bofu-map/). If it ends in a slide and not a changed URL, you held a meeting.

## Turn the loop into a page a buyer can use

Each pass should land on one bottom-of-funnel page: alternatives, versus, pricing questions, best-for, switching, or integrations. Pick the URL that matches what you just heard, and change four things.

- The opening situation, so the right reader recognizes their week in the first lines.
- The objection you kept hearing, answered in public, including where you are the wrong fit.
- One specific from the visit or from using the product. A step, a limit, a dependency, a timeframe with the condition attached.
- A sentence that still stands if an answer engine lifts it out of the page. A slogan next to a logo will not get cited, and a champion cannot forward it.

The person comparing options at night will not book a call so you can discover their problem. They already described it. The page is the proof you heard them.

Run it again when the answer sounds like an old deal. The problem moves as you scale: the risk they defend, the alternatives they name, the colleague they must convince. A visit shows that. A guess does not.

## A cadence a small team can keep

A small team can keep this without a research department. The busy month is when the guess creeps back, so the rhythm has to be small enough to survive it.

- Two buyer conversations a month. One win and one loss. Twenty minutes. The same three questions, so you can see what changed.
- One hour a month in the product, doing a task a new customer does in week one. Write down where you hesitated.
- One visit or one industry event a quarter, with a single question you want answered in their words.
- One page updated within a week of each conversation. Not a new essay. The money page that was thin.

Share the phrases with whoever writes and whoever sells, dated. When the homepage, the comparison, and the call describe three different problems, buyers get lost, and so do the tools describing you from those pages.

## What to do this week

Open the comparison or alternatives page with the most serious traffic. Highlight every sentence you cannot trace to a conversation, a support ticket, or time in the product from the last six months. If most of the page lights up, start there.

Book two conversations this week. Ask what they searched, what almost stopped the deal, and which line they skipped. Put one phrase in the first paragraph and answer that objection above the call to action. Ship the edit before you draft anything new. A quiet site can still make that call. [Website decisions with only hundreds of visitors](/blog/website-decisions-with-low-traffic/) come from recordings, sales questions and a handful of people looking at the page, not from a test you cannot power.

## Common mistakes

- **One research project, then a year of guessing.** The loop is the habit. A deck from last spring is a souvenir.
- **Talking only to people who already love you.** Losses tell you what the page failed to answer. Happy customers tell you what worked after they had decided.
- **Collecting quotes and changing nothing.** If no URL changed, you have notes, not a loop.
