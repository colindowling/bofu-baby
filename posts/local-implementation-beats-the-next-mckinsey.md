Trying to be the next McKinsey is a positioning trap. The buyer in front of you is not hiring a global brand. They have a local job, a local constraint, and a desk the work has to sit on.

An operator we interviewed was clear about where the work actually is. A lot of it is going to be local. You have to be able to say you can help the local car dealership. You can help the dental shop that, right now, probably has no way to query its own records through a local model, because of HIPAA. The implementation is on the ground: come in with a Mac Mini, set it on the desk, and be able to say the data never leaves right here.

That is not a small-market anecdote. It is the shape of a page that converts. The McKinsey-shaped page says you do transformation. The useful page says who it is for, what is stuck, and what never leaves the building.

## The big-firm page does not match the search

"Next McKinsey" copy is easy to write and impossible to win with. It sounds like every other firm that discovered the same phrase. The headline is a category. The proof is a logo wall or a claim about senior people. Nothing on the page is a job a specific operator has this week.

Buyers do not search "next McKinsey for AI." They search the situation. A dealership that cannot get a straight answer out of its own numbers. A dental office that will not send records to a vendor because of HIPAA. A team that needs the model in the building, not in a slide about the future of professional services.

If your comparison page, your alternatives page, and your best-for page are written in the big-firm voice, you miss those searches. You also hand the citation to whoever was willing to be specific. Answer engines do not repeat "we sit at the intersection of strategy and implementation." They repeat a sentence a buyer could have asked. [Sounding like everyone else](/blog/me-too-positioning/) is how you become the page nobody quotes.

The fear underneath the McKinsey pose is that a narrower page shrinks the market. It does the opposite. [A narrower market is easier to be found in](/blog/afraid-to-niche-down/). The dealership and the dental shop are not the whole economy. They are proof you know one job well enough to describe it. Other buyers with the same shape of problem recognize themselves. The buyers who wanted a global brand were never going to hire you from a page anyway.

## Put the implementation on the page

On the ground means the reader can picture the afternoon. Not a framework. A desk.

For a page like this, four lines do more than a positioning statement:

- **Who.** The local operator. A dealership. A dental shop. Whoever actually has the stuck job. Not "the enterprise."
- **What they cannot do today.** Query their own records. Get an answer without emailing three people. Trust a tool that wants the data in someone else's cloud.
- **The constraint, in their word.** HIPAA, or whatever rule makes a normal vendor pitch a non-starter. Do not bury it. It is the reason they are still searching.
- **The physical proof.** A local model. A Mac Mini on the desk. The sentence "the data never leaves right here." If you cannot say that sentence about your own offer, do not borrow it. Say the true version, including where the data does go.

That last point is where technical readers decide if you are serious. [They dismiss a page that will not be specific](/blog/content-technical-buyers-wont-dismiss/). "Local LLM" as a badge is fluff. "The model runs on a machine in the office, and the records do not leave that machine" is a claim they can test. Put the testable sentence above the call to action.

These are bottom-of-funnel pages, not a manifesto. The list is short: alternatives, versus, best-for, switching, integrations, and the questions they ask before they book. That list is the [BoFu Map](/bofu-map/). One URL for the dealership's job. One URL for the clinic that will not move the records. Each page gets one implementation, one constraint, and one place you are the wrong fit. The McKinsey page can stay off the site.

A champion has to explain you to someone who was not on the call. "They put a machine on the desk and the data stays here" survives that explanation. "They are the next McKinsey" does not. Write the sentence the champion can forward. That is also the sentence an answer engine can cite when someone asks who can do this without the data leaving the office.

## What to do this week

Take the page that currently sounds the most like a big firm. Read the headline and the first screen. If you could swap in another firm's name and nothing would break, it is a McKinsey page.

Replace the headline with one operator and one stuck job. Add the constraint in their word. Add the implementation in physical terms, and the limit: who this is wrong for. Cut every line that only says you are senior, strategic, or full-service.

Then search the phrase a local buyer would actually type, including the constraint. If your page is not a reasonable result for that phrase, you have a topic. You do not have a page yet.

## Common mistakes

- **Borrowing the big-firm costume.** Buyers can tell. So can the models that summarize you from your own site.
- **Using the local story as color, then pitching the platform.** The dealership and the dental shop are the point. If the page leaves them in the first paragraph, you walked back into the trap.
- **Skipping the constraint.** HIPAA, or whatever rule stops the obvious vendor, is the search. A page that never says it will not be found by the buyer who is stuck on it.
