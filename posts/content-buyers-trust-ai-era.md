To make content buyers still trust when everyone assumes AI wrote it, put in what a model can't know: real experience, specific examples, honest trade-offs and a point of view. Use AI for speed, keep the judgment human, and aim fewer, sharper pieces at buyers who are close to a decision.

I heard this from two directions recently. One operator said content-led outreach is losing its power, because people now assume AI must have done the work for you. A marketing leader put the other half of it well: it's easier than ever to produce content, and harder than ever to do it well.

Both are true at once. That's the tension.

## What changed

A few years ago, a thoughtful article showed effort. Somebody sat down and wrote it. That effort was a signal.

Now anyone can produce a decent-sounding article in a minute. So the signal is gone. A well-structured post on a common topic proves nothing anymore, and buyers know it. When they see one, they assume it's generic, and they move on.

The result is a lot of content that technically exists and practically doesn't.

## What buyers still reward

Buyers haven't stopped reading. They've gotten pickier. They reward things that only a person with experience could write:

- **Specific situations.** "A mid-size team on this CRM hits this problem at about this size."
- **Real numbers.** Costs, timelines, results, even rough ones.
- **Honest trade-offs.** Where your approach isn't the best fit.
- **A point of view.** Something you believe that others in your space don't.
- **Mistakes.** What you got wrong and what you learned.

None of those can be generated from what's already online. That's the point.

## Use AI for the right parts

I'm not against using AI for content. It's useful for research, outlines, first drafts, editing and turning one piece into five. Refusing to use it just makes you slower.

The line I'd draw is this: AI can help with the writing. It can't supply the experience. So keep humans responsible for:

- The idea and the angle.
- The examples and the numbers.
- The accuracy.
- The final voice.

If a piece has none of those from a real person, it's probably not worth publishing.

## Write closer to the decision

Most AI-generated content clusters at the top of the funnel: "what is X," "10 trends in Y." That's exactly where the flood is.

Bottom-of-funnel content is harder to fake. A useful comparison page needs someone who knows both products. A good pricing explanation needs someone who knows how deals actually get priced. A switching guide needs someone who's watched teams switch.

Those pages also reach buyers who are ready to decide, so the trust you build there turns into pipeline.

## Publish less, make it better

If you're publishing four generic posts a week, try one specific one instead. Measure it on qualified leads, not page views. You'll likely find that fewer, better pieces do more, both with buyers and with the AI tools that now recommend vendors.

## Don't hide behind the brand

One more thing. Content from a named person with real experience earns more trust than content from "the team." Put the expert's name on it. Let their voice come through. Buyers want to know who's talking.

## A quick before-and-after

Here's the kind of opening that reads as generic: "In today's fast-paced business environment, companies face growing pressure to do more with less." Every word is true. None of it is useful.

Here's one that reads as written by someone who knows: "If your team is reconciling the same report in two systems every Monday, the problem usually isn't the report. It's that nobody owns the data in between." Same topic. One of them earns a second paragraph.

## What to do this week

Pull your five most recent posts. For each, highlight every sentence a competitor could publish unchanged. If more than half the post is highlighted, rewrite the opening with one specific situation from a real customer conversation, and add one honest trade-off. Then compare how those revised pages perform over the next month against the ones you left alone.

## A note on volume

Publishing less feels risky. It isn't, if each page answers a real buying question better than anything else out there.
