You define the problem you solve by asking customers what was happening right before they started looking, and writing it down in their words. If your team can't name that problem clearly, your content will be vague, buyers won't recognize themselves in it, and it won't rank for the searches they actually make.

An operator I talked with recently, someone who has worked inside many growing companies, said it directly: a lot of times the companies, the marketers and the salespeople aren't really clear about the problem they're solving.

That's a bigger deal than it sounds. Everything in marketing hangs off that one answer.

## Why teams lose the problem

It's not that teams don't know their product. They know it too well. Over time, they start describing what it does instead of why anyone needed it.

The homepage says "the all-in-one platform for modern teams." Sales talks about features. Marketing writes about trends. Nobody says, plainly, "you're losing a day a week to this, and here's how to stop."

## Why it hurts your content

Buyers don't search for your product category's tagline. They search for their problem, or for comparisons once they know what they need:

- "how to stop [specific pain]"
- "[competitor] alternative"
- "best [category] for [their situation]"
- "[category] pricing"

If your pages talk about features and vision, they won't match those searches. And when buyers ask an AI tool, it can't confidently recommend a company whose own site doesn't say clearly who it helps and with what.

## Find the problem in your customers' words

You don't brainstorm this in a conference room. You go find it.

### Ask recent customers

Three questions do most of the work:

- What was happening right before you started looking?
- What did you try first?
- Why did you pick us?

Write down the exact words they use. Don't clean them up.

### Mine what you already have

Sales call recordings, support tickets, reviews, and the questions prospects ask in demos are full of problem language. Look for phrases that repeat.

### Check what people search

Look at the search terms that bring people to your site and your competitors' sites. The bottom-of-funnel ones, comparisons and alternatives, tell you what buyers are weighing.

## Write it down in one sentence

Once you've collected the language, write one sentence: "[This kind of company] struggles with [this problem], which costs them [this]."

If it sounds a little uncomfortable, a little too honest, you're probably close. The real problem is usually less flattering than the one on your homepage.

## Build pages around it

Now every bottom-of-funnel page gets a simple rule: one page, one problem, one reader. A comparison page names the problem the buyer is solving and shows how each option handles it. An alternatives page speaks to people frustrated with a specific tool, about the specific thing that frustrates them.

Pages built this way do two jobs. They match what buyers search. And when buyers land, they recognize themselves right away.

## Check the whole team says it the same way

Last step. Ask five people in your company, from sales, marketing and leadership, what problem you solve. If you get five answers, you have work to do. When the answers line up, your content, your sales calls and the AI tools describing you will start to line up too.

## Signs you haven't named the problem

- Your homepage headline describes your product, not the buyer's pain.
- Your content gets traffic but almost nobody books a call.
- Sales spends the first ten minutes of every call explaining what you do.

## A worked example

Say a software company's homepage reads "The modern platform for operations teams." In interviews, customers keep saying some version of "we were losing track of approvals across three systems." That second sentence is the problem. A page titled "How to stop losing approvals across systems," written for operations leads at mid-size companies, will match real searches and make sense to the reader immediately. The platform is still the answer. It just stops being the headline.

## What to do this week

Ask five customers the three questions above. Write one sentence from their answers.

## Common mistakes

- **Cleaning up the customer's words.** Their rough phrasing is what other buyers search for.
- **Naming three problems at once.** One page, one problem, one reader.
